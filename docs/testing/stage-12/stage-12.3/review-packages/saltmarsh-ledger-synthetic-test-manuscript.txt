Chapter 1

Wren Halloway stepped off the ferry, her boots crunching on the gravel path leading up to the old chandlery. The sun was setting, casting long shadows across the salt-marsh, and she felt a chill despite the warmth of the day. She had returned to Greymouth, the town where she had grown up, to take over the chandlery that had been her aunt’s for as long as she could remember. The building stood silent and uninviting, its windows dark and its doors shut tight.

Her aunt, Eliza, had died just weeks ago, leaving Wren with more than just the chandlery. She had left behind a legacy of secrets and unanswered questions. Wren had always known her aunt to be a woman of few words, but now it seemed her silence had become permanent. The ledger, bound in leather and locked away in a chest in the attic, was the only tangible connection to the past she could find. It was a small comfort, a weighty reminder of what she was expected to do.

Wren had never known her father; her aunt had only ever said he was a sailor, and that the sea had a long memory and a short temper.

As she approached the chandlery, Wren noticed that the sign above the door was missing, its wooden letters rotting away. She reached up and pulled the door open, stepping inside. The air was thick with the scent of old wood and something else—something faintly familiar, almost like the tang of salt water. The interior was dim, the light from outside barely filtering through the dirty windows. Dust motes danced in the beams of light, a testament to the years of neglect that had passed since anyone had last tended to the place.

Wren moved through the dusty space, her eyes adjusting to the gloom. The shelves were lined with bottles of oil, candles, and other nautical supplies. Some of the jars were empty, their contents long since used up. A sense of melancholy washed over her as she realized how much work lay ahead. But there was also a spark of determination. She would restore this place to its former glory, just as she had promised her aunt.

She made her way to the back of the shop, where a narrow staircase led up to the attic. The steps creaked under her weight, and she paused at the top, taking a deep breath before pushing open the trapdoor. The attic was cluttered, filled with boxes and old furniture. In one corner, a large chest sat, its lid slightly ajar. Wren approached it, her heart pounding with a mix of curiosity and dread. She lifted the lid, revealing a stack of ledgers, each bound in brown leather. The oldest looked like it might crumble at the touch.

She pulled out the topmost ledger, its pages yellowed and brittle. As she flipped through it, she found herself drawn into the details of daily life in Greymouth, the names and dates, the transactions and deliveries. Each entry was a piece of a puzzle, a fragment of the past that needed to be pieced together. Wren knew that these ledgers held the key to understanding her aunt’s life and the history of the chandlery.

As the sun dipped below the horizon, casting the town in a golden glow, Wren decided to leave the attic for now. She would spend the evening sorting through the items in the chandlery, making a list of what needed repair and what could be sold. She needed to clear some space and get a better look at the ledgers, perhaps find a clue as to why her aunt had left them here.

Outside, the marshes whispered secrets to the wind, and the tide began to ebb. Wren closed the door of the chandlery behind her, locking it for the night. She walked along the quayside, feeling the cool sea breeze against her face. The town was quiet, the lights of the houses flickering on as the night settled in. She thought about the journey ahead, the challenges she would face, and the stories waiting to be uncovered. Greymouth, with its salty air and hidden histories, was ready to welcome her back, and she was ready to uncover its secrets.

Chapter 2

Wren Halloway stepped out of the drab, rain-soaked street into the chandlery’s bustling interior. The air was thick with the scent of tar and the sound of mending nets. Ada Pell, a wiry woman with hands that moved with practiced ease, looked up from her work at the long table where she sat. Her eyes, sharp and observant, locked onto Wren’s as she stood there, her own hands resting on the cool wooden counter.

“Wren Halloway, I presume,” Ada said, her voice carrying a hint of accent from the marshlands. “You’re new here, aren’t you?”

The chandlery's own ledger was so swollen with seawater that Ada laughed and said a second ledger would have to be started, and they hung the first one to dry beside the altar candles Wren's aunt had kept for the chapel.

Wren nodded, her gaze darting around the cluttered space filled with barrels, ropes, and various nautical supplies. “Yes, I am. I’ve been tasked with sorting through the damage from the storm.”

Ada’s eyes flickered with interest. “Aye, that’s no small task. You’ll need to be thorough. Follow me.” She gestured towards a row of wooden benches along one wall, each holding a variety of nets and lines. Wren followed, feeling a mix of apprehension and determination.

As they approached the first bench, Ada began to lay out the nets, spreading them out carefully. “This here is a common cod net,” she explained, pointing to a large, intricately woven mesh. “And this,” she continued, turning to another, “is a mackerel gillnet. Each has its purpose, and it takes years to learn the difference between them.”

Wren listened intently, her fingers trailing lightly over the coarse fabric. “I see how the different sizes and weaves catch different fish. It must be quite a skill.”

Ada smiled, her eyes twinkling with pride. “It is indeed. Now, let’s start with what’s salvageable. This net here,” she picked up a tattered piece, “will need more than just a few stitches. It’s beyond repair. We’ll have to cut it up and use the material for smaller jobs.”

They worked in silence for a while, Ada’s deft hands repairing the nets while Wren watched, learning by osmosis. The rhythmic clacking of the mending needles against the wood was soothing, a steady pulse of the chandlery’s life.

As they progressed, Ada turned to Wren, her tone becoming more serious. “Tell me, Wren, why did you come to Greymouth? What brought you to us?”

Wren hesitated, then decided to be honest. “I was looking for something—someone—to help me solve a mystery. My sister went missing, and I believe her disappearance is connected to some of the activities in this harbour.”

Ada’s expression softened. “I understand. Loss can drive a person to do things they might not normally consider. But you must be careful. These waters can be treacherous, and the people who call them home are not always the most trustworthy.”

Wren nodded, her mind racing with thoughts of her sister and the dangers that lurked in the shadows of Greymouth. “I know. That’s why I’m here. To find answers and bring my sister home.”

Ada placed a hand on Wren’s shoulder, her grip firm but gentle. “Then you’re in good company. We’ve seen our share of mysteries and disappearances. If anyone can help you, it’s the men and women of this chandlery.”

They continued their work, the sun creeping through the windows and casting a warm glow over the room. As the day wore on, Wren found herself more at ease, the routine of the chandlery grounding her amidst the chaos of her search. The nets, though damaged, held a sense of order, a promise of renewal in the face of loss.

Chapter 3

Wren Halloway stood at the window of the chandlery, her fingers drumming against the glass as she watched the bustling harbor. The salty breeze carried the scent of seaweed and brine, and the distant cries of gulls added to the symphony of the sea. She had been waiting for Edric Thorne, the stern-looking harbourmaster, to arrive, hoping he might have some information about the missing ledger.

Edric Thorne strode into the chandlery with a purposeful step, his weathered face and sturdy frame making him an imposing figure. He nodded curtly to Wren without breaking stride, his gaze immediately locking onto the stack of unpaid dues sitting on the counter. "Miss Halloway," he said, his voice low and authoritative. "I trust you've prepared these payments for me?"

On the harbour wall Captain Abel Ferris tipped his cap to Wren, as he did to everyone, and went on mending his pipe.

Wren turned from the window, smoothing her hands over the neat pile of coins and bills. "Yes, Mr. Thorne. They're all here." She gestured to the stack, her heart pounding slightly under the scrutiny of his eyes. She knew she was being tested, but she was determined to meet his expectations.

"Very well," he said, his tone firm. "Let us see them then." Edric Thorne took the stack from Wren and began to count out the money meticulously. His hands were calloused and strong, each movement precise. Wren watched him, noting the way his brow furrowed slightly as he checked the totals.

"There's a discrepancy," he remarked, his voice steady but unyielding. "These do not add up correctly."

Wren's breath caught in her throat. "I... I'm sorry, Mr. Thorne. I must have made a mistake." She reached for the ledger, flipping through the pages anxiously. "Let me check again."

Edric Thorne held up a hand, halting her. "No need. I can see it. The figures don't match what should be owed." He placed the stack back on the counter and leaned forward, his eyes narrowing. "This is not the first time this has happened, Miss Halloway. You know the consequences if you continue to neglect your duties."

Wren felt a wave of shame wash over her. She knew the risks, but she had been so focused on the ledger that she had let her attention slip. "I understand, Mr. Thorne. It won't happen again. I'll make sure to double-check everything from now on."

He stood up straight, his expression softening slightly. "That will be acceptable for now, but you must ensure that it does not occur again. These matters are too important to be left to chance." Edric Thorne turned to leave, his steps resolute.

As the door clicked shut behind him, Wren let out a long breath. She felt a mix of relief and guilt. She had been so close to uncovering something significant with the ledger, but now she had to focus on ensuring her work was flawless. She picked up the ledger and examined it once more, determined to find the error and correct it before anyone else noticed.

Wren sat down at her desk, the weight of the ledger heavy in her hands. She flipped through the pages, her mind racing. The ledger was old, its pages yellowed and brittle, but the entries were clear. She had transcribed every transaction meticulously, yet there was a discrepancy that eluded her. She ran her fingers over the entries, searching for any sign of a mistake, but found none.

She decided to start from the beginning, going through each entry carefully. As she did, a memory surfaced, a conversation she had overheard between two sailors. They had been discussing a ship that had recently arrived, a ship that had not paid the usual fees. Wren's heart skipped a beat. Could the ledger have been tampered with? She quickly turned to the entry for that ship, her fingers tracing the inked lines.

There, in the margin, she found a small, almost imperceptible alteration. Someone had changed the amount owed, reducing it significantly. Wren's mind raced as she realized the implications. Whoever had done this had likely done it to cover their tracks, perhaps to avoid paying the proper fees. But why? And who could it be?

Wren's thoughts were interrupted by the sound of footsteps outside. She looked up to see Mr. Thorne re-entering the chandlery, his expression unreadable. "Miss Halloway," he said, his voice low and serious, "there is another matter I need to discuss with you."

Wren felt a chill run down her spine. She had suspected that her oversight with the ledger might attract unwanted attention, but she hadn't expected it to come so soon. She sat up straighter, her heart pounding. "Of course, Mr. Thorne. What is it?"

He approached her desk and gestured to the ledger. "This," he said, pointing to the altered entry, "is not the only discrepancy I have noticed. There are others, and they are becoming increasingly difficult to ignore." His eyes met hers, and she saw a flicker of something that was not quite anger but certainly concern.

Wren's mind raced, trying to piece together the puzzle. She had to act fast if she wanted to protect herself and the truth behind the ledger. "Mr. Thorne, I assure you that I am doing my best to rectify these issues. Perhaps we should look into it further together."

He considered her words for a moment, his expression still unreadable. "Very well," he said finally. "But you must be more vigilant in the future. This is not a game, Miss Halloway. Lives depend on the accuracy of these records."

Wren nodded, her resolve hardening. She would not let this opportunity slip away. She would find the truth, no matter how dangerous it might be. As Edric Thorne left the chandlery once more, Wren picked up the ledger, her fingers trembling slightly. She had a lot of work to do, but she was ready to face whatever challenges lay ahead.

Chapter 4

She could feel the tension in her muscles as she braced herself for the work ahead.

Wren nodded, taking off her coat and tying it around her waist. She grabbed a rope and began to help the fishermen haul their boats up the shingle, each movement precise and practiced. The sound of the wind was almost deafening, but she could hear the shouts and commands from Thorne, the harbor master, echoing through the mist.

Between orders, Thorne grumbled that the foxglove in the chapel garden was choking the herbs again, and poured himself more tea from the tin pot he carried everywhere, tea so strong it could have poisoned a mule.

Thorne, a tall man with a weathered face and a deep, gravelly voice, stood at the head of the dock, barking out instructions. "Move faster! We need to get these boats under cover before the storm hits!" His words were sharp and clear, cutting through the chaos. Wren and the other women heaved with all their might, the ropes digging into their palms as they pulled the heavy boats up the slippery slope. The men, seasoned and hardened by years at sea, worked alongside them, their faces etched with determination.

As they hauled the last boat to safety, Wren caught a glimpse of Thorne’s anxious expression. He was scanning the horizon, his eyes searching for signs of the coming storm. "We’ve got to move," he shouted over the wind, his voice strained. "Get everyone to the shelter of the warehouse. We can’t afford to lose any more boats."

Wren turned to Ada, who was already leading several of the younger women towards the warehouse. "Come on," she said, her voice urgent. "Let’s get the rest of them moving." They moved quickly, their steps hurried as they guided the workers towards the protection of the warehouse. The sound of the wind grew louder, the rain harder, but Wren felt a sense of purpose as she helped her fellow townspeople find safety.

Outside, the storm raged on, the wind howling and the rain pelting down in sheets. But inside the warehouse, the women huddled together, their voices rising in a chorus of conversation and laughter. Despite the chaos outside, there was a sense of calm, a shared understanding that they would weather this storm together. As the night wore on, Wren felt a growing sense of peace, knowing that she was part of something larger than herself—a community that faced challenges head-on and stood strong in the face of adversity.

Chapter 5

Wren Halloway stood at the customs office door, her fingers drumming impatiently against the frame. The wooden panels were worn and scarred, a testament to the countless transactions that had passed through this very spot. She took a deep breath, trying to calm her nerves as she pushed open the door and stepped into the dimly lit room.

Inside, the air was thick with the scent of salt and old paper. A large oak desk dominated the space, behind which sat an older man with a stern expression. His name badge read "Silas Crane, Customs Officer." Wren approached him, her heart pounding in her chest.

In the end Crane agreed to forgive her aunt's duties if Wren reported any boat landing after dark at the chandlery steps, and she signed, telling herself she would never have to keep her word.

"Good morning, Officer Crane," she said, extending her hand. "I’m here regarding my aunt’s unpaid duties."

Officer Crane looked up from his paperwork, his eyes narrowing slightly. "Ah, Miss Halloway. I see we have a bit of a situation here. Your aunt, Eliza, has been owing quite a sum for some time now."

Wren nodded, her shoulders sagging slightly under the weight of the conversation. "Yes, I understand. But I assure you, it’s not intentional. My aunt has been... unwell."

Silas Crane leaned back in his chair, his gaze piercing. "Unwell, you say? And how does that excuse your aunt’s failure to pay her duties?"

Wren hesitated, choosing her words carefully. "She’s been dealing with a... a serious illness. It’s been difficult to manage everything, including the duties."

Officer Crane tapped his pen against his desk, clearly unconvinced. "I see. Well, you’re here now. What can you offer me to settle this matter?"

Wren pulled out a small leather-bound ledger from her bag, its pages filled with meticulous entries. "This is my aunt’s ledger. It shows all the transactions and payments she made. I believe there might be a miscalculation somewhere."

Crane snatched the ledger from her hands, flipping through the pages with a critical eye. "Let me see this," he muttered, his brow furrowing. He paused at a particular entry, his finger tracing the ink. "This one, for instance. It looks like there was a discrepancy in the tax assessment."

Wren nodded eagerly. "Exactly! I’ve been trying to sort this out, but it’s been challenging without her help."

Crane looked up, his eyes meeting hers. "Very well, Miss Halloway. I’ll review this ledger and see if there’s any merit to your claim. However, I must insist that you keep up with your duties henceforth. No more arrears."

Wren smiled gratefully. "Thank you, Officer Crane. I promise to do better. If there is any way I can repay what is owed..."

Crane waved her off. "No need for repayment. Just ensure it doesn’t happen again. Now, let me see... I think I’ve found the issue. This will require a small adjustment to your account."

Wren felt a wave of relief wash over her. "That’s wonderful news. Thank you so much."

As they continued their discussion, Wren couldn’t help but notice the way Crane’s eyes seemed to linger on certain entries in the ledger. There was something almost... curious about the way he was examining them. She wondered if he suspected more than just a simple miscalculation.

After a few minutes, Crane finally closed the ledger with a sigh. "It seems the discrepancy was due to a clerical error. Your aunt’s duties are now adjusted. You may leave, Miss Halloway."

Wren gathered her things, her spirits lifted. As she turned to leave, she caught a glimpse of Crane’s face in the mirror hanging above his desk. His expression was unreadable, but there was a faint smile playing at the corners of his mouth. She couldn’t shake the feeling that there was more to this encounter than met the eye.

Stepping out into the sunlight, Wren felt a sense of accomplishment. The customs office door clicked shut behind her, leaving her to ponder the implications of her discovery. Little did she know, the ledger held secrets that would soon unravel the mysteries of Greymouth’s hidden past.

Chapter 6

Wren couldn’t help but smile back, feeling a sense of camaraderie with Liesel. She had been a fixture in Greymouth since before Wren could remember, a source of both comfort and scandal. “Thank you, Liesel. What are we celebrating?”

As they took their seats at one of the small round tables, Wren noticed the other guests—mostly women, some men, all drawn by the promise of a good story. Stepping outside, the cool air of the marsh greeted her, the salty breeze carrying the distant sound of waves crashing against the shore. Wren walked along the path, her thoughts swirling with the tales of the wrecks, wondering what other secrets the sea held, and whether she might be the one to uncover them.

Chapter 7

Wren Halloway sat at the small oak table in the attic, surrounded by the musty scent of old books and papers. The room was dimly lit by a single candle, casting flickering shadows across the walls. She had been searching through her aunt’s belongings for hours, her fingers tracing the worn leather of the ledger she had found earlier that day. The pages were brittle and yellowed, but still legible.

As she flipped through the pages, her heart sank when she noticed a peculiar pattern. Several entries from the past few months were missing, replaced by blank spaces. Wren felt a chill run down her spine as she realized what this could mean. Her aunt, Amelia Halloway, had been meticulous with her records, yet these gaps were too deliberate, too suspicious.

“Why would she tear out those pages?” Wren whispered to herself, her mind racing with possibilities. She remembered how her aunt had always seemed secretive, especially around certain topics. But why now? And who could she be hiding information from?

Determined to uncover the truth, Wren began to piece together the dates of the missing entries. They spanned several weeks, covering a period just before her aunt had disappeared. The entries were about financial transactions, shipping records, and even mentions of a mysterious figure named “Ethan.”

She pulled out a notebook and began to make notes, each one a step closer to understanding what her aunt had been up to. Wren’s hands trembled slightly as she wrote, the ink smudging on the paper. She wondered if her aunt had been involved in something illegal, something that could have endangered her life.

Her thoughts were interrupted by a soft knock on the attic door. Startled, Wren looked up to see her cousin, Tom, standing in the doorway. His face was serious, and he held a small, leather-bound journal in his hand.

“Wren, I found this in the kitchen,” he said, handing her the journal. “It looks like it might belong to your aunt.”

Wren took the journal and opened it carefully, her eyes scanning the pages. They were filled with detailed sketches of ships, maps of the local harbor, and notes about the tides and weather patterns. There were also references to the same “Ethan” mentioned in the ledger.

“This is different,” Wren murmured, her voice barely above a whisper. “This seems more personal, less like business.”

Tom leaned against the doorframe, his expression concerned. “I think we should tell Father about this. He might know more.”

Wren nodded, her mind already racing with new questions. She knew her father, Nathaniel, had been suspicious of his sister ever since she had disappeared. But could he be the one behind her aunt’s disappearance? Or was there someone else entirely?

She tucked the journal into her pocket and stood up, her legs feeling unsteady. “Let’s go talk to him,” she said, her voice steady despite the turmoil inside her.

Together, they descended the stairs, the creaking wood echoing through the old house. The air downstairs was cooler, and the smell of salt and seaweed filled the hallways. Wren and Tom made their way to the study where her father usually worked, though today it was unusually quiet.

Nathaniel sat at his desk, staring at a stack of papers that seemed to stretch endlessly. He looked up as they entered, his expression unreadable.

“What is it, Wren?” he asked, his voice flat.

Wren hesitated, then took a deep breath. “I found something that might be important. It’s about Aunt Amelia and Ethan.”

Nathaniel’s eyes narrowed, and he set down the papers he was holding. “What do you have?”

Wren handed him the ledger and the journal. Nathaniel’s brow furrowed as he flipped through the pages, his fingers moving swiftly over the text.

“Where did you find these?” he asked, his tone sharp.

“In the attic. I was going through some of Aunt Amelia’s things.”

Nathaniel sighed, running a hand through his hair. “This is going to change everything. We need to be careful. There are people who would do anything to keep these secrets buried.”

Wren nodded, her heart pounding. “Do you know who Ethan is?”

Nathaniel’s gaze hardened. “I don’t know much, but I can tell you this: Amelia wasn’t just keeping records for her own sake. She was doing something important, something that could put us all in danger.”

Wren felt a shiver run down her spine. “So, you think someone is after her?”

Nathaniel’s eyes met hers, and she saw a glint of fear. “It’s possible. We need to be prepared. I’ll start looking into this right away.”

Wren watched as her father left the study, his steps resolute. She felt a mix of fear and determination wash over her. Whatever was happening, she was determined to find out the truth, no matter the cost.

Chapter 8

The sky was a dull grey as Wren and Ada made their way through the narrow streets of Greymouth, the air heavy with the scent of salt and seaweed. The town was still asleep, most of its inhabitants tucked away in their homes against the chill of the early morning. Wren followed Ada down the cobbled path, her boots crunching softly on the stones. Her heart pounded with a mixture of excitement and trepidation, the kind that comes from stepping into the unknown.

As they reached the edge of the marsh, Ada paused and turned to face Wren. "This is where we’ll start," she said, her voice barely above a whisper. "The tide’s going out, so we can see the paths clearly." Wren nodded, her eyes scanning the expanse of mud and water before them. The marsh stretched out like an endless sea, its surface broken by the occasional gull or the shimmer of sunlight filtering through the fog.

Ada swore the old smugglers' tunnels had all collapsed before her grandmother's time, and that anyone who talked about passwords and secret doors under the Anchor Inn had been reading penny novels.

Ada led the way, her feet moving confidently across the uneven terrain. Wren followed closely, her eyes fixed on the ground, searching for any sign of the old smugglers’ paths. The marsh was a labyrinth of salt-tainted earth and twisted reeds, each step revealing new patterns and textures. Ada pointed to a series of faint lines etched into the mud, almost invisible but unmistakable to someone who knew the land well.

"These are the old paths," Ada explained, her voice steady despite the mist that seemed to hang heavier around them. "They were used by smugglers to transport goods from the boats to the mainland. It’s a dangerous business, but it’s how people survived in these parts." Wren listened intently, her mind racing with the stories Ada was weaving. She imagined the secret meetings, the whispered plans, the risk of discovery.

The old rope-works pier had been half-buried in silt since the fire, and Ada said nothing worth finding had ever been buried there except broken bottles.

They walked for what felt like hours, the landscape shifting subtly under their feet. The sun began to rise, casting long shadows and turning the marsh into a canvas of light and shadow. Ada stopped suddenly, her hand resting on a weathered wooden post. "Here,” she said, gesturing to a small, crumbling structure. “This is where the rope-works stood.”

Wren approached the remains, her hands brushing against the rough planks. The structure had been a hive of activity once, with ropes and nets hanging from every beam.
